Abracadabra

eleven lines · sixty-six letters · one point

  1. Prepare

    Sit somewhere quiet. Bring to mind the one thing you want to wane: a fear, a habit, a heaviness, a bad week. Name it here, or just hold it.

    It stays on this screen. Nothing is saved or sent.

  2. Write

    Type the word, letter by letter. Every line loses its last letter, so the word narrows to a point. As each line closes, say it aloud, a little softer each time.

  3. Seal

    At the point one letter is left, and then nothing. What you named went down with the word. The triangle closes and its light goes up.

  4. Close & return

    The old way: print it, fold it, tie it with a thread and wear it nine days. Then throw it backwards over your shoulder, without looking, into a stream that runs east.

Abracadabra

where the word comes from and how it works

The first instructions

The oldest written instructions come from a Roman book of remedies set down in verse in the early third century. Write the word on a sheet, it says, then write it again beneath, each time taking one letter away from the end, until the lines narrow to a point like a cone. Tie it with linen and hang it at the neck. It was written against fevers and against whatever clings.

Why it narrows

The thing named wanes with the word. Each line holds a little less of it; at the point a single letter remains, and after it, nothing. This is the oldest kind of shape-magic: the form itself does the work. Practitioners call it waning, or diminishing.

The word itself

No one knows for certain where it comes from. It is often read as Aramaic, avra kedabra, “I create as I speak.” Others hear Abraxas, the great power whose seven Greek letters add up to 365, one for each day of the year. Others find the Hebrew for Father, Son and Holy Spirit folded inside it.

Reading the triangle

Eleven lines, sixty-six letters. The left edge reads A all the way down. The right edge reads ARBADACARBA: the word in a mirror, written down the side of itself.

Down the centuries

The triangle was copied into charm books for over a thousand years, and Londoners wore it on their chests through the plague year of 1665. Later books add the closing this page teaches: wear it nine days, then cast it backwards over your shoulder into a stream that flows east, and walk away without looking back.

Working it on paper

Use one sheet and dark ink. Write the full word on the first line, and on each line below drop the last letter, keeping the lines centred so the shape narrows evenly. Say each line as you finish it. Don’t cross anything out; if a line goes wrong, start a fresh sheet. Fold it so the writing is hidden, tie it, and keep it against your body.

The Seven Laws of the Long Night

  1. The cold and the poisoned air strike first. Put a battery CO alarm on each level. Run any generator outdoors, at least 20 ft from doors, windows and vents. Never heat a home with a gas oven or range.
  2. One gallon, every person, every day. For drinking and sanitation. Do not ration drinking water: drink what you need today and find more for tomorrow.
  3. No water is clean until the rite is done. Rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 5,000 ft), or unscented bleach at 8 drops per gallon for 6% (6 drops for 8.25%), then wait 30 minutes.
  4. Stop the blood before anything else. Firm pressure. For life-threatening limb bleeding, a tourniquet 2–3 in above the wound, never over the elbow or knee. Write down the time.
  5. Learn the lookalikes before the feast. Water hemlock, poison hemlock, death camas and the deadly Amanitas first. Then the edibles.
  6. Low-acid food is pressure-canned or not canned at all. Meat, vegetables and milk need 240–250°F (10–15 psi). Botulinum spores survive boiling.
  7. The circle is stronger than the bunker. Neighbours who already know each other recover better than isolated households with more gear.

Commit these to memory. Everything else in this book is commentary on them.

Prologue: What the Dark Actually Does

In real American grid failures, most people die from cold, carbon monoxide and lost medical care. Violence and starvation are not the main killers. A survival plan should start with body temperature, safe heat and medicine, then water, and only then food and craft skills. In Texas's February 2021 storm, 161 of 246 confirmed deaths (65.4%) were cold exposure and another 19 were carbon monoxide poisoning (Texas DSHS). After Hurricane Maria, Puerto Rican households averaged 84 days without power and 68 without water, and about a third of the estimated 4,645 excess deaths came from interrupted health care (Fortune; Healthcare Dive on NEJM). Those numbers set the order of this handbook. It starts with priorities and safe heat. Then it covers water treatment with exact bleach doses, fire and light, and shelters from a debris hut to adobe and earth lodges. It goes on to region-by-region wild foods with their deadly lookalikes, first aid that works without a hospital, and making cordage, soap, pottery, tools and preserved food. It closes with sundials and navigation, sanitation, community, and growing food for the long term. The strongest single finding across disaster research is that neighbors who already know each other recover better than isolated households with more gear (Aldrich, Natural Hazards 2011). Skills, relationships and printed references are worth more than an arsenal. Practice the primitive methods here before you need them, because a first attempt in an emergency usually fails.

The real kill order in US outages

The popular "rule of threes" says you can last 3 minutes without air, 3 hours exposed in harsh weather, 3 days without water and 3 weeks without food. It has no documented origin, and its numbers are not real limits. Most people can hold their breath for only 30–90 seconds, and the shelter figure applies only to harsh conditions (Wikipedia: Rule of threes). Use it as a ranking, not a timer: air (including CO-free air), then body temperature, then water, then food. For American households, put medical continuity near the top. That means insulin, oxygen, dialysis and prescriptions, and the Maria data shows why (Harvard GHELI).

Death tolls come in late and low. Texas's count rose from 57 to 246 over months (Texas DSHS). The quiet killers were hypothermia, CO, failed medical equipment, lack of home oxygen, falls and fires (NPR).

STOP before you act A

The US Forest Service teaches STOP (USFS: If You Get Lost):

  1. Stop. Stay calm and stay put. The USFS calls panic "your greatest enemy."
  2. Think. Work out how you got here and what you have.
  3. Observe. Get your bearings and take stock. Don't wander.
  4. Plan. Think through several plans, then act on one. If you are injured, exhausted, or it is nightfall, stay where you are.

The Army's joint survival checklist also opens with "Size up the situation, surroundings, physical condition, equipment" (FM 21-76-1). Real panic is rare in disasters (Quarantelli 2008). The more common failure is freezing in place. Research by John Leach is often summarized this way: roughly 10–15% of people act effectively in a sudden emergency, most are "stunned and bewildered," and a minority act counterproductively. The exact percentages vary by source (BPS Research Digest). Written, rehearsed household plans are the countermeasure.

Carbon monoxide. Never use generators, grills, camp stoves or any gasoline-, propane- or charcoal-burning device inside a home, basement, garage, tent or camper. Staying cool in a summer outage A

Extreme heat means temperatures above 90°F for at least 2–3 days. Fans "do not reduce body temperature or prevent heat-related illnesses" (Ready.gov Heat). EPA advises against using electric fans for cooling once room temperatures reach the mid-90s °F (EPA). What works: cool showers or baths, wet cloths on the skin, loose light-colored clothing, rest during midday, plenty of non-alcoholic fluids, a cooling center if one exists, and twice-daily checks on elderly neighbors and young children (CDC Power Failures in Hot Weather). Practical additions C: pick the lowest, north-side room, close sun-facing blinds by day, and open windows at night. Heat roughly doubles water needs (Ready.gov Water).

Stay home or leave?

Official guidance treats sheltering in place as the default when leaving is riskier (Ready Illinois). Evacuate when officials order it. Mobile and manufactured homes should be evacuated in hurricanes (FEMA). Choose your destination ahead of time (Salt Lake County EM). Red Cross shelters generally do not admit pets, though service animals are allowed (DisasterAssistance.gov).

No authoritative source gives criteria for when to leave during a long collapse. This handbook's synthesis C is to leave early, to a pre-arranged destination, when:

  • the home cannot be kept at a safe temperature,
  • medical needs such as dialysis or oxygen cannot be met,
  • fire or flood threatens, or
  • officials order evacuation.

The Waters

Water: settle, filter, then boil or chlorinate
XIV · Temperance🜄Water☽Moon

Water is the first mirror and the first poison. Settle it, filter it, then give it to fire or to chlorine. Nothing that looks clear is clean until the rite is done.

How much you need A

Plan on 1 gallon per person per day for drinking and sanitation. An active adult drinks about ¾ gallon of that, and hot weather can double it. Do not ration drinking water. Drink what you need today and find more for tomorrow. Limit exertion, and avoid caffeine and alcohol, which increase dehydration (Ready.gov Water). A household of four needs 56 gallons for two weeks at baseline, and more in a Southern summer.

Signs of dehydration in infants include a dry mouth, crying without tears, and lack of urination (MedlinePlus). Sources disagree on how many hours without a wet diaper is serious: 6, 8 or 12 (Alberta MyHealth; Harvard Health). Treat any long dry stretch in a baby as urgent.

Water already inside your house A

The water heater tank is the biggest reserve, roughly 30–45 gallons. To drain it:

  1. Shut off the water supply and the heater's power or gas.
  2. Let the water cool.
  3. Open the drain valve at the bottom and turn on a hot-water tap to let air in.

(CDC; Red Cross checklist)

To drain house pipes, shut the main valve, open the highest faucet to let air in, and drain from the lowest faucet (Utah Be Ready). Ice cubes made from safe water count too.

Toilet tank water (the tank, never the bowl) is usable if it is clear and has no colored cleaner, by CDC's standard. Several local agencies say to use it for sanitation only. Treat it before drinking (CDC).

Never drink from radiators, hot-water heating boilers or waterbeds, which can contain algicides (Utah Be Ready; Kearney NE). Shutting the house main early, before the municipal supply can become contaminated, protects what is in your heater and pipes C.

Rain catchment

A useful rule of thumb from simple arithmetic: 1 inch of rain on 1 sq ft of roof yields about 0.62 gallon. That means a 1,000 sq ft roof gives about 620 gallons per inch of rain. Divert the first flow, which carries roof debris and droppings. Oregon State Extension says to divert at least 10 gallons per 1,000 sq ft of roof for every inch of rain A (OSU EM 9101). One manufacturer says to divert up to 50 gal per 1,000 sq ft from roofs dirty with leaves or bird droppings B (Rainwater Equipment brochure). All rainwater must still be treated.

Finding water in the wild

Terrain signs (ranked by this handbook, mostly B). Use them in this order:

  1. Low ground. Look in drainages and low spots. In a dry wash, dig at the outside bend, which is deeper, shadier, and the last place water flowed (Backpacker).
  2. Water-loving trees. Cottonwood, sycamore, willow and tamarisk grow near water (Phoenix FD via Yahoo).
  3. Animals. Game trails that converge downhill often lead to water, and desert birds fly to water at dawn and dusk (Kenyon College). Trails can also lead nowhere (Scouting Magazine).
  4. Insects are the least reliable sign.

The USFS says to follow a drainage downhill only as a last resort when lost (USFS).

Transpiration bag B. Fill a clear plastic bag ½–¾ full of green, leafy, non-poisonous vegetation on a sunny slope. Remove sticks and spines, add a small rock to make a low point, and seal the bag. Several bags on broadleaf plants beat one dug solar still for the effort involved. A solar still yields only about 0.5–1 liter per 24 hours (landsurvival.com FM 21-76 excerpt). A 2025 review found stills only "marginally useful" (medRxiv preprint). Neither method meets daily needs.

Plant water B.

  • Wild grapevine: in spring and early summer, cut it at ground level and notch it high up. It flows for 20–30 minutes.
  • Maple and birch sap: runs roughly January to early March. Any maple except Norway maple works; Norway maple has milky sap.
  • Identify every plant with certainty first, because some vines and trees have toxic sap (OffGrid Web; Outdoor Life).
✦ take care

Barrel cactus is not a canteen. The National Park Service says the idea has "little basis in fact." The Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum says fishhook barrel cactus moisture contains oxalic acid and is likely to cause diarrhea, which worsens dehydration. (NPS; ASDM)

Purification: exact doses

Boiling A is the best method. It kills bacteria, viruses and protozoa.

  • Bring water to a rolling boil for 1 minute.
  • At altitude, boil 3 minutes. EPA sets the altitude threshold at 5,000 ft and CDC at 6,500 ft. Use 3 minutes above 5,000 ft to be safe.
  • Settle cloudy water first, then strain it through cloth or a coffee filter.
  • To fix the flat taste, add a pinch of salt per quart or pour the water back and forth between clean containers.

(EPA; CDC)

Household bleach A: use only regular, unscented bleach of 6% or 8.25% strength that is less than a year old. Never use scented, color-safe or "plus cleaner" bleach.

  1. Add the dose. Double it if the water is cloudy, colored or very cold.
  2. Stir and wait 30 minutes.
  3. Smell the water. It should have a faint chlorine smell. If it doesn't, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.
  4. If there is still no smell, discard the water (Ready.gov).

CDC's equivalent for 5–9% bleach is 8 drops (about 0.5 mL) per gallon and 40 drops (2.5 mL, ½ tsp) per 5 gallons. For 1% bleach, use 40 drops (½ tsp) per gallon (CDC). Keep an eyedropper or a 60 mL syringe in your kit, because drop sizes vary.

Pool shock (calcium hypochlorite, HTH) A. One package treats thousands of gallons, but it is a strong oxidizer.

  1. Work outdoors or somewhere ventilated, and wear eye protection.
  2. Dissolve 1 heaping teaspoon (about ¼ oz) in 2 gallons of water to make a stock solution of roughly 500 mg/L.
  3. Treat water at 1 part stock to 100 parts water, which is about 1 pint of stock per 12.5 gallons.

(EPA)

Iodine A: use 2% tincture at 5 drops per quart, or 10 drops if the water is cloudy, and wait 30 minutes. Iodine does not kill Cryptosporidium. Avoid it if you are pregnant or have thyroid problems, and no one should drink iodine-treated water for more than a few weeks (EPA; CDC).

Chlorine dioxide tablets do kill Crypto if you follow the label's dose and wait time (CDC).

Solar disinfection (SODIS) A:

  • Use clear PET plastic bottles of 3 liters or less, and replace badly scratched ones.
  • Check clarity: set the filled bottle on a newspaper headline and look down through the neck. If you can't read the letters, filter the water first.
  • Lay the bottles in full sun for at least 6 hours, or 2 consecutive days if more than half the sky is cloudy. SODIS fails in prolonged rain.
  • Drink straight from the bottle.

(SODIS/Eawag; CDC)

Filters: what they do and don't remove

CDC's criteria A:

  • To remove bacteria, a filter needs an absolute pore size of 0.3 µm or smaller.
  • To remove Giardia and Crypto, it needs 1 µm or smaller.
  • Portable filters do not remove viruses, so disinfect afterward (CDC).

The Sawyer Squeeze (0.1 µm absolute) and LifeStraw (0.2 µm) remove bacteria and protozoa, but not viruses (Sawyer; LifeStraw). Keep hollow-fiber filters from freezing, because a frozen filter may no longer work reliably (OutdoorGearLab).

Sari cloth filter A. In a trial across 65 Bangladeshi villages, filtering through old sari cloth folded 4–8 times cut cholera by 48% (Colwell et al., PNAS 2003). It is a pre-filter, not purification.

Improvised sand and charcoal filter B. Layer it coarse to fine in the direction the water flows: cloth or grass, then gravel, then sand, then crushed homemade hardwood charcoal, with cloth over the outlet (Wilderness Awareness School). One guide puts the charcoal above the sand instead, so sources disagree (Battlbox). Never use store briquettes. It only clarifies water, so boil or disinfect afterward.

Making charcoal for filters and fuel A/B.

  • Heat hardwood above about 400°C (750°F) with little oxygen (Virginia Tech Extension).
  • Practical method: pack wood into a 1-gallon paint can or small drum with a small vent, set it inside a larger drum, and burn scrap wood in the gap.
  • Seal it and let it cool overnight before opening. Hot char reignites when air reaches it (SurvivalBlog).
  • A double-barrel retort yields about 10–15 lb per batch (Oklahoma State Extension).

Long-term household filters A.

  • Biosand filters remove about 98–99% of bacteria and over 99.9% of protozoa (Wikipedia: Biosand). They keep about 5 cm of standing water over a biological layer. Rest them between pours, and swirl-and-dump the top when flow falls below 0.1 L/min. They take days to weeks to mature (SSWM). Build from the CAWST Biosand Filter Manual v10.
  • Ceramic pot filters are local clay mixed with sawdust or husks that burn out to leave pores, often treated with colloidal silver. They flow at 1–3 L/hour (CDC slides; Global Ecovillage Network).

Chemical contamination: when to walk away A

Boiling and disinfecting cannot make water safe if it contains fuel, toxic chemicals or radioactive material, so find another source (CDC). They also do not remove heavy metals or salts (EPA). Treat floodwater as chemically contaminated. Avoid water downstream of farms, mines, roads and industry C.

Distillation removes salts and metals. Fill a pot halfway, tie a cup to the lid handle so it hangs upright inside when the lid is inverted, and boil for 20 minutes. The water that condenses and drips into the cup is distilled (Ready.gov). Ready.gov confirms the halfway fill and the 20-minute boil; the cup detail is standard practice.

✦ take care

Farm-country wells and infants. Boiling concentrates nitrate. Infants must not drink water, including formula water, above 10 mg/L nitrate-nitrogen. The risk is "blue baby" syndrome. Bluish-brown skin in an infant needs a hospital. (CSU Extension; Minnesota DOH; Illinois DPH)

Storage A

  • Use food-grade containers. Wash them with dish soap, rinse, then sanitize with 1 tsp unscented bleach per quart of water.
  • Store them cool and dark.
  • Replace self-stored water every 6 months (Ready.gov).
  • In a long outage, re-treat any stored water of uncertain age with the bleach dose above C.

Method ladder

Primitive friction fire is a skill you practice, not an emergency plan. This handbook's order of reliability C is:

  1. lighters and matches (stockpile heavily),
  2. ferro rod,
  3. battery and steel wool,
  4. lens (sunny days only),
  5. flint and steel with char cloth,
  6. bow drill,
  7. hand drill and fire plough.

In one experimenter's 370 trials, the hand drill averaged 4.0 out of 5 for effort against 3.2 for the bow drill (Primitive Ways).

Bow drill, step by step A/B

Part Dimensions
Fireboard (soft, dry, dead, non-resinous wood) About 2.5 cm (1 in) thick, 2.5–10 cm wide, 15–45 cm long
Spindle About 2 cm thick (⅜–1 in) and 15–30 cm long (about half your knee-to-ground height). Blunt bottom; top tapered to a rounded tip about ¼ in across
Bow About arm length, ⅜–1 in thick, slightly flexible, with a tight cord about ⅛ in thick (leather, cotton, sinew)
Socket (handhold) Hardwood such as oak, hickory or maple, about 6 × 2 in, or a stone with a ⅜ in depression

Sources: FM 3-05.70 Ch. 7; Rannie, NIU "Fire by Friction".

  1. With a knife point, start a pit about ⅛ in wide and deep, one spindle-width in from the board edge. "Burn in" the pit with a few strokes until it is nearly flat.
  2. Cut a notch from the board edge into the pit, reaching slightly past the pit's center. Rannie prefers parallel sides and the Army uses a V; both work. Without a notch, embers form only 28% of the time (Primitive Ways).
  3. Put a thin bark "coal catcher" chip under the notch.
  4. Grease the top of the spindle and the socket with skin oil, fat or soap. Never use water there. You want heat only at the bottom.
  5. For a right-hander: kneel on the right knee and pin the board with the left foot. Lock the left wrist against the left shin to hold the spindle vertical. Wrap the cord once, with the spindle on the outside of the cord.
  6. Saw with long strokes parallel to the board. Start slow and light, then add speed and pressure. When the notch fills with smoking dark powder, finish with a fast burst.
  7. Stop and hold still for a few seconds. Roll the board away while pinning the coal with a stick. Don't tap it loose.
  8. Tip the coal into a loose tinder nest about 4 in across. With your back to the wind, blow long and steady, holding the nest at eye level or above.

(Rannie; FM 3-05.70)

Troubleshooting:

  • Spindle drills through the board: flatten the spindle bottom.
  • Spindle walks off the board: you are bowing at an angle.
  • No ember: the notch is missing or too small.
  • Coarse or gritty dust: reject that wood. Good wood grinds to fine dark powder (Rannie; Primitive Ways).
  • Cord slips: tighten the bow C.
  • Pit is shiny or glazed: add a pinch of sand C.
  • Brown dust instead of black: add speed and pressure C.

Avoid wood lying on the ground. It often seems dry but "lacks the friction needed" (FM 3-05.70).

Other ignition methods

  • Flint and steel A/B. Strike carbon steel (stainless won't spark well) against flint, quartz or chert, with char cloth laid on top of the stone. Drop the glowing cloth into a tinder nest and blow (FM 3-05.70; Wilderness Awareness). Keep your knuckles clear of the sharp edge.
  • Char cloth B. 1. Punch a 1/16 in hole in a mint tin and burn the tin out once. 2. Load it with 8–10 loose pieces of 100% cotton, about 2 × 3 in. Linen and jute also work. 3. Set it on hot coals and turn it a few times. 4. When flame from the hole stops and the smoke dwindles to a whiff, remove the tin. 5. Let it cool for 5 minutes before opening.

Good cloth is uniformly black, so re-cook anything brown (Wilderness Awareness).

  • Ferro rod A. Lay a dry leaf under the tinder, rest the rod on it, and scrape sparks onto the tinder (FM 3-05.70).
  • Battery and steel wool A/B. Touch fine #0000 steel wool across both terminals of a 9V battery (Instructables). Store 9V batteries taped or capped, because loose 9Vs touching metal have started house fires (Snopes).
  • Lens A/B. Use a magnifier, binocular lens or scope in bright sun on dark tinder. An ice lens needs clear, bubble-free ice and is a last-ditch trick (FM 3-05.70; Primitive Ways).
  • Fire piston B. A greased, perfectly sealed piston compresses air to ignite char cloth or amadou (Wilderness Awareness).
  • Gunpowder A, last resort. Pull a bullet and use the powder as an accelerant. Discard the casing, because primers stay sensitive (FM 3-05.70).

Tinder, kindling and wet-weather fire

Fire succeeds or fails on preparation. You need bone-dry fine tinder, graded kindling from pencil-lead to thumb thickness, and an armload of split fuel gathered before you strike (FM 3-05.70; Field & Stream).

  • Natural tinders B: juniper bark; inner bark of cedar, cottonwood, tulip poplar or pawpaw; dry grass; dried flower heads; aster and cattail down (as an additive, not alone); dogbane fiber. Rub them fluffy and keep the finest fibers for the center of the nest (Wilderness Awareness; Outdoor Life). Learn poison ivy, oak and sumac before you gather.
  • Amadou (Fomes fomentarius, horse-hoof fungus) B. It grows on birch and beech across the northern US. 1. Separate the suede-like middle layer. 2. Boil it about 20 minutes in weak wood-ash water. 3. Rinse it, pound it thin with a wooden mallet, and dry it.

It catches sparks and smolders for a long time, so it can carry fire between camps (Wilderness Awareness). Ötzi the Iceman carried it 5,000 years ago (Wikipedia: Amadou).

  • Fatwood B. Resin-soaked pine heartwood from old dead stumps, taproots and branch knots. It is heavy, golden to red-brown, and smells strongly of turpentine. Split into the stump to reach it (Make:). It is the best wet-weather starter in pine country.
  • In the rain B:
    • Split wood to reach the dry interior.
    • Harvest standing dead branches. Use only sticks that snap cleanly; bending means wet.
    • Carve feather sticks.
    • On snow or wet ground, build on a platform of green logs laid side by side (FM 3-05.70; Outdoor Life).

Banking and carrying fire. Bank coals to keep them until morning, and carry lit punk or fungus when you move (FM 3-05.70). Making light

Rendering tallow B. Chop the suet (partly frozen fat is easier to cut), melt it slowly with or without a little water, and strain out the solids (Prairie Homestead).

Tallow or beeswax candles B.

  1. Melt the fat in a can set in hot water.
  2. Center a cotton wick with a clothespin.
  3. Let the wax cool slightly, then pour it into jars, or make tapers by repeated dipping with cooling between dips.
  4. Trim the wick to about ¼ in. Too small a wick tunnels; too large a wick smokes.

Pure tallow is soft, so blend in beeswax where you have it. Well-made tallow candles stayed sound after more than a year of storage (Prairie Homestead; Grit).

Rushlights B. This is the cheapest historical light.

  1. Peel a rush, leaving one narrow strip of skin for strength.
  2. Dry the pith.
  3. Draw it once or a few times through melted tallow, bacon grease or beeswax.
  4. Burn it clamped at an angle in a holder.

(Wikipedia: Rushlight; Sew Historically)

Oil lamp B. Use a heat-safe dish of vegetable oil or melted fat with a cotton wick held up by a wire or metal tab, so the burning tip sits just above the oil. Olive oil burns cleanest. Thick oils wick poorly, so keep the wick short and the oil level high (Primal Survivor; Mother Earth News).

Pitch or fatwood torches C are smoky and drip burning resin. Use them outdoors only.

Plan your work around daylight. Keep LED lights, rechargeable batteries and a small solar panel as your main lighting, with flames as backup C.

Emergency shelters

Your clothing is your first shelter. The most important rule is to insulate yourself from the ground with leaves, pine needles or boughs (FM 21-76; Survival.powweb).

Debris hut A/B. This is the best quick warm shelter when you have only your hands.

  1. Rest a long ridgepole from the ground up to a stump or tree fork, at about body length plus a little.
  2. Lean ribs along both sides.
  3. Pile light, dry, soft debris over the frame. One guide says to make it at least 3 ft thick.
  4. Stuff the inside with more dry debris.

(FM 21-76 via Survival.powweb)

Lean-to and tarp A-frame A/C. Face the back of the shelter into the wind (FM 21-76). Build a fire with a reflector wall on the open side. A tarp A-frame follows the same rules: a ridgeline, the closed end to the wind, and insulation underneath C.

Quinzhee B.

  1. Pile snow into a mound.
  2. Let it set.
  3. Hollow it out.
  4. Poke a vent hole and keep it open, especially while snow is falling.

Never seal the entrance completely, and sleep with your face near the vent. Vent-size advice conflicts. One source scales it from 4 in at 30°F down to 1 in at −30°F, while others say fist-sized or about 6 in (SectionHiker; Wilderness College).

Igloo B. Cut wind-packed snow into blocks and lay them in a ring. Cut a ramp through the first course so the blocks rise in a spiral. Bevel each block to lean inward, pack the gaps, and finish with a capstone. Keep a vent high on the wall (MDC Xplor; Popular Science).

✦ take care

Snow shelters collapse during building, when temperatures rise, or when someone climbs on them. Don't build one alone. Use no open flame inside. Cook outdoors and use LED light. A sealed snow dome can kill by suffocation or CO. (SectionHiker; Popular Science)

Desert shade shelter B. Build it only at dawn or dusk, because digging in the heat costs water.

  1. Dig a trench about 1.5–2 ft deep to reach cooler soil.
  2. Roof it with a double layer with about 12 in of air space between the layers to block radiant heat.
  3. Use a reflective emergency blanket as the outer layer if you have one.

Avoid rock overhangs, which shelter scorpions and rattlesnakes, and check for ant mounds (Battlbox; Aircav Army aviation survival text).

Brush shade (ramada or brush hogan) B/C. Navajo builders distinguish a brush or summer hogan, a shade shelter, from the earth-covered winter hogan (UNM Hillerman collection). An open shade roof on posts suits hot, humid summers.

Read your soil first

Every earth building method depends on the mix of clay (binder), sand and gravel (aggregate) and, sometimes, straw (fiber). Too much clay cracks and too much sand crumbles (NPS Tumacácori adobe lesson).

Jar test A.

  1. Fill a jar about ⅓ with soil and top it up with water.
  2. Shake hard for 2–3 minutes.
  3. Watch it settle. Sand drops first, then silt, and clay forms the top layer.
  4. Measure each layer's thickness as a share of the total.

NMSU reads a quick result after 1 hour (NMSU G-521). Clemson lets fine clay settle for about 48 hours (Clemson HGIC).

Jar tests can mislead, and even 2–5% clay changes how a soil behaves. Always confirm with hand tests and 3–5 test bricks of different mixes. Dry them, then reject mixes that crack (too much clay) or crumble (too much sand) B (GreenBuildingAdvisor).

Adobe brick, step by step A

NMSU Extension Guide G-521 is the best primary how-to (NMSU G-521).

Soil: use loamy sand, sandy loam or sandy clay loam. Sand at least 50% and clay never more than 30%. Straw is optional and not structurally necessary. NMSU prefers adding sand, because straw can attract insects and rot. That conflicts with a common practitioner starting mix of about 30% clay to 70% aggregate including straw B (Permies). Let your test bricks decide.

  1. Dig a mixing pit about 2 ft deep, or use a tarp or wheelbarrow. Soak the pit for at least 12 hours.
  2. Add soil, then water a little at a time, mixing until it is stiff.
  3. Sprinkle sand on level drying ground.
  4. Wet the forms, fill them, and pack the corners hard.
  5. Strike off the top with a straight edge and lift the form gently.
  6. Wash the form and refill it.
  7. Dry the bricks at least 3 days before handling. Turn them on edge after several days, and allow 3–4 weeks before rough handling.
  8. Cover stacks if showers threaten. Build in the long dry season (May–August in most of New Mexico). Freezing during drying destroys bricks (Masonry Design excerpt).

Cob, rammed earth, wattle and daub, earthbag, sod, cordwood

  • Cob B. Mix just enough clay to bind the sand, and enough sand to stop cracking. Tread it barefoot or in flat boots.
    • Build in lifts of 10–24 in, letting each dry before the next. Use clay slip as glue between lifts (Mother Earth News).
    • The old West Country rule is "good boots and a good hat": a raised, dry stone foundation about 300 mm wider than the wall, and a roof overhang of at least 30 cm.
    • Never render cob with cement, which traps moisture. Use lime plaster (LowImpact cob factsheet).
    • The 2021 International Residential Code added an optional cob appendix (AU), which applies only where a jurisdiction adopts it (ICC).
  • Rammed earth A/B. Tamp damp sandy or gravelly soil into temporary forms in layers of 7.5–15 cm. Lime was a historical stabilizer (Auroville).
  • Wattle and daub A. Weave a lattice of sticks or reeds and plaster both sides with daub, a mix of clay, soil, sand, water and straw (Designing Buildings).
  • Earthbag / SuperAdobe A/B. Fill long fabric tubes or bags with site soil, lay them in courses, and tamp each course. Put barbed wire between courses. It needs only hand tools and makes curved domes (Cal-Earth). It is the most forgiving method for beginners. Plaster the bags, because polypropylene breaks down in UV light C. Cal-Earth's stabilized system has ICC code recognition, and commercial use needs a license (Cal-Earth).
  • Sod house (Great Plains) A. Cut blocks ("prairie marble") from sod-forming grasses with dense roots, such as buffalo grass, slough grass or big bluestem. Bunchgrasses don't hold together. Lay the blocks grass-side down on leveled, undisturbed soil, two blocks wide, with staggered joints. Sod walls insulate against heat and cold. A plowed, back-burned firebreak around the house protected against prairie fire (Encyclopedia of the Great Plains).
  • Cordwood B. Lay short log ends crosswise in mortar. A common 16 in wall has 3–4 in mortar beads on each face with insulation between them. A sawdust-lime fill at 12:1 is reported at about R-3 per inch. Cob mortar can replace cement (Northern Woodlands; Mother Earth News).

Builders from around the world

  • Icelandic turf house B. A timber frame sits on flat stones. The walls are two courses of turf blocks with gravel or earth filling the cavity between them (Hurstwic).
  • Nubian vault B. Courses of mud brick lean against a gable wall, so no wooden formwork is needed. Ramesseum granary vaults have stood for more than 3,500 years. Spans are limited to about 3.3 m, and the method is not suited to high-rainfall regions (ArchDaily; AFD guide).
  • Mongolian ger (yurt) B. Collapsible lattice wall sections (khana) support straight roof poles (uni) running to a crown ring (toono). It is covered with 3–5 layers of felt under a waterproof shell and held together by the covers' weight and tension bands (National Geographic Education). It is a strong portable design for the Plains and Mountain West.
  • Irish clochán (corbelled dry stone) B. Each course steps slightly inward until the courses meet, so no centering is needed. Walls are about 1–2 m thick (Columbia University). It suits treeless, stony ground, with heavy labor.

Plasters, floors and thatch

Earthen plaster B. Finish coat: 1 clay : 2–3 sand : 0–½ straw, or 2–2.5 fine sand : 1 clay : ¼ flour paste. Leave straw out of the finish coat. If it cracks, add sand; if it won't stick, add clay. A little lime per wheelbarrow load deters mold (Natural Building Blog; Dancing Rabbit).

Lime plaster B. Use 1 lime putty : 2.5–3 sharp sand. Historic "1:3" recipes often meant quicklime, which swells when slaked, so the real mix was nearer 1:1.4 (GreenSpec; Building Conservation). Slake quicklime and store it as putty; don't store quicklime itself.

✦ take care

Slaking quicklime throws off heat, spits, and makes caustic dust. Add lime to water, never water to lime. Wear goggles, gloves, long sleeves and a dust mask, and keep plenty of water for eye flushing. (LowImpact; Building Conservation)

Earthen floor B.

  1. Lay a gravel layer, then a sand-clay-straw base.
  2. Add a 1 in finish of 6 sand : 2 clay : 1 chopped straw, and burnish it when nearly dry.
  3. Let each layer dry for 4–5 days, and mist it before adding the next.
  4. Seal with several coats of linseed oil, thinned progressively: 100%, then 80/20, then 60/40 oil to thinner.

(Dancing Rabbit; LowImpact/Sigi Koko)

Thatch B.

  • Pitch: 45° minimum, about 50° typical. Steeper lasts longer.
  • Bundles (yealms): 36–48 in long, about 6 in thick at a tie 12 in from the butt.
  • Thickness: new grass or straw goes on 15–20 cm (6–8 in); long water reed goes on 280–330 mm.
  • Fixing: start at the eaves and work up.

(Designing Buildings; Small Farmer's Journal; FAO)

US substitutes include common reed (Phragmites), cattail, big bluestem and palmetto C. Keep thatch well clear of chimneys and smoke holes C.

Heat, cooling and passive solar

The DOE passive-solar rule is to collect sun through south-facing openings and store it in thermal mass. Use overhangs to shade south glass at the June solstice while letting in full sun at the December solstice. In hot climates, favor north-facing windows and shade the south heavily (DOE Energy Saver; GBA).

For a hand-built shelter C:

  • run the long axis east to west,
  • put most openings on the south,
  • berm the north side, and
  • put the earthen floor or interior adobe wall where the sun strikes.

Thermal mass is not insulation. In cold climates, wrap mass inside an insulating layer. In the desert, combine bare mass walls with night ventilation C.

Rocket mass heaters run hot exhaust through a cob bench that stores the heat (Wikipedia). They have no tested safety standard, and practitioners dispute whether cracked burn chambers leak CO. Every fuel-burning shelter needs a CO alarm (Permies).

Mushrooms: mostly don't A

From November 2025 to March 2026, Northern California recorded 39 suspected amatoxin poisonings, 4 deaths and 3 liver transplants, all from foraged mushrooms. It was the largest US outbreak of its kind in decades. CDC's advice is to avoid foraging wild mushrooms, especially in rainy seasons (CDC MMWR 75(20)). Amatoxin symptoms are delayed, then come a false recovery and liver failure. Without a transplant center, a single mistake is likely fatal, and mushrooms supply few calories.

If you forage anyway, use only these distinctive species and their checks:

  • True morel: slice it lengthwise. It must be hollow from cap tip to stem base with the cap fused to the stem (the half-free morel is the one exception). Always cook it, and eat a small amount the first time (MDC). Treat all false morels (Gyromitra, Verpa) as toxic. Gyromitra stays toxic after normal cooking (MDC).
  • Giant puffball: slice every one top to bottom. The inside must be uniformly white with no outline of a cap or stem, because young destroying angels look like puffballs (MDC).
  • Chanterelle: it has blunt, forked false ridges running down the stem that can't be plucked off individually. Jack-o'-lanterns have sharp true gills and grow in clusters on wood (UF/IFAS; Messiah Univ.).
  • Chicken of the woods: cook thoroughly, eat only the young tender margins, and try a small portion first. Raw, old or undercooked specimens have caused GI illness (UF/IFAS; MykoWeb).

The Universal Edibility Test: last resort only B

The Army's test exposes skin, then lip, then tongue to each part of a plant separately, with waits of about 4 hours, before chewing and swallowing tiny amounts over roughly a day (Art of Manliness). Its flaws:

  • It cannot detect delayed toxins, so there is no safe way to taste-test hemlock (Greenbelly).
  • It rejects bitter staples like acorns.
  • It costs most of a day per plant part.

Survival instructor Paul Kirtley calls promoting it as a foraging method "dangerous" (Kirtley). Never use it on mushrooms, carrot-family plants or milky-sap plants. The real answer is learning your local plants now.

Low-tech protein

  • Insects B. Eat brown, black, green or tan species, such as grasshoppers, crickets, grubs and ants. Avoid brightly colored ones, like the Southeast's eastern lubber grasshopper. Catch grasshoppers in the cold early morning. Remove heads, legs and entrails, and always cook them (Field & Stream; Backpacker).
  • Fishing devices (Army manual) B (Prepare-and-Protect, FM 21-76):
    • Hooks from pins, wire, thorns, bone or shell. For a wooden hook, cut a hardwood shank about 2.5 cm long and 6 mm thick, notch one end, and lash in a point.
    • The gorge: a sliver sharp at both ends, tied at the middle and hidden lengthwise in bait.
    • The stakeout: two saplings driven into the bottom with a cord strung between them just underwater, carrying short baited lines.
    • Funnel fish baskets of sticks lashed with vine.
    • Tidal stone traps: choose the site at high tide and build at low tide.
    • Spearing in waist-deep water: pin the fish to the bottom, don't throw, and allow for refraction.
  • Trapping and set lines are heavily restricted by state law in normal times, and deadfalls kill pets. Learn your state's rules and practice only where legal C.

Wild meat. - Cook all wild game to at least 165°F (74°C), checked with a thermometer. Meat color is unreliable (CDC MMWR 2024). - Wild Trichinella, mostly from bear, survives freezing, salting, drying and smoking (CDC). - Rabbits cause about 90% of US tularemia, mostly during skinning (Montana FWP). Proposed safe ceilings are about 25–35% of calories from protein, or 2–2.5 g/kg/day (Hektoen International; Wikipedia citing Bilsborough & Mann). The Healer's Hand

Medicine and first aid: stop bleeding, keep wounds open, cool or warm the core
XVII · The Star🜄Water♀Venus

The healer's first art is pressure, the second is clean water, the third is patience. Herbs are allies at the bedside, never a substitute for the hand that stops the blood.

◉ Ouija Cat, by telepathy

Herbs are lovely. Bleeding is faster. Pressure first, poultice later. Write the time on the tourniquet, not on your little vision board.

What herbs cannot treat

No herb treats sepsis, appendicitis, major trauma, anaphylaxis or severe dehydration in infants.

Sepsis warning signs A: fever or shivering or feeling very cold, extreme pain, clammy skin, confusion, shortness of breath, and a high heart rate or weak pulse. "No single sign" confirms it, so treat a worsening infection as an emergency (CDC).

Appendicitis A: pain that starts around the navel and moves to the lower right abdomen, getting worse with coughing, walking or jarring. It can be milder in older adults and in pregnancy (Mayo Clinic; MSD Manual). Get these people to any surviving medical care, however far away.

Bleeding control A

  1. Apply firm direct pressure.
  2. Pack deep wounds not on a limb. Stuff gauze tightly into the wound and hold pressure (Florida DOH Stop the Bleed).
  3. Tourniquet for life-threatening limb bleeding. Place it 2–3 in above the wound, never over the elbow or knee, and tighten it until the bleeding stops. Write down the time (Stop the Bleed / USUHS; LAFD).
  4. If one tourniquet fails, put a second one about 2 in above the first (Vanderbilt).
  5. An improvised tourniquet should be non-elastic fabric at least 2 in (5 cm) wide. Belts, wires and cables are unlikely to work (Cleveland Clinic). Stockpile a commercial windlass tourniquet.

Wounds: clean, then usually leave open A

  • Irrigate generously with drinkable tap water, or with boiled-and-cooled or distilled water. A Cochrane review found tap water performs about as well as saline (PubMed 36103365).
  • Never close an infected wound. Avoid closing contaminated wounds or clean wounds more than 6 hours old, and plan a delayed closure instead (WHO).
  • Hesperian advises leaving small wounds to heal alone and not closing deep wounds that can't be closed in layers (Hesperian).
  • Without antibiotics, a trapped infection can become fatal sepsis. Pack and dress wounds open rather than sewing them shut.
  • Sterilize instruments that cut skin or touch blood by boiling or steaming them for 20 minutes, counted from a full boil (Hesperian).
  • Signs of infection include spreading redness, warmth, pus, red streaks and swollen glands C.

Burns

Cool the burn with clean, cool water. A commonly taught figure is 10–20 minutes C. Use no ice and no butter, and don't pop blisters C. Then dress it. Honey has high-quality evidence for partial-thickness burns. In a Cochrane review it healed them about 4.7 days faster than conventional dressings (Cochrane CD005083). The trials mostly used medical-grade honey. Never give honey to infants under 1 year, because of botulism spores C. Topical aloe gel may speed burn healing and ease pain (NCCIH).

Hypothermia (WMS 2019) A

Outside a hospital, judge the stage by signs, because field thermometers are unreliable.

  • Leave wet clothes on until the person is somewhere warm.
  • Check skin under heat packs every 20–30 minutes for burns.
  • Small chemical packs can't rewarm the core.
  • Rough handling, walking, or warming the arms and legs can cause a dangerous "afterdrop."
  • Fixed pupils and rigor are not reliable signs of death in a cold patient.

Frostbite A

Rewarm in water at 37–39°C (99–102°F) for about 30 minutes, until the tissue is soft (WMS 2024). Do this only if the part will not refreeze. Refreezing does more damage than leaving it frozen (AAFP). Don't rub it C. The 2019-era WMS guidance allowed field ibuprofen up to 600 mg four times a day. Check that dose against current guidance.

Heat stroke A

Cool first, transport second. Cold-water immersion is the gold standard and drops core temperature roughly 1°C every 5 minutes. Stop at about 39°C (102°F) rectal. If full immersion is impossible, lay the person on a tarp, pull up the edges, and fill it with water (Korey Stringer Institute; Loyola).

Diarrhea and oral rehydration A

Homemade ORS: 1 liter of boiled, cooled water + 6 level teaspoons sugar + ½ level teaspoon salt (Rehydration Project; WHO EMRO).

  • An older formula used 8 tsp sugar and 1 tsp salt, so sources conflict. Standardize on the 6 : ½ recipe and use level measuring spoons. Too much salt is dangerous for children.
  • It should taste no saltier than tears. Discard it after 24 hours (unofficial handout).
  • Commercial ORS packets are better.
  • For cholera, avoid juice, soda and sports drinks, which can worsen diarrhea (CDC).

Fractures, sprains and anaphylaxis A

Fractures: don't try to realign a bone or push one back in. Splint the joint above and the joint below, and pad the splint (Mayo Clinic). Check circulation, sensation and movement below the injury before and after splinting. Loosen the splint if fingers or toes go pale, cold, numb or blue (MedlinePlus). For sprains, use cold and elevation (UMass Memorial).

Anaphylaxis: use the person's epinephrine auto-injector. Give a second dose if symptoms persist after 5–10 minutes, and keep the person sitting or lying down (American Red Cross). Stockpile two injectors for anyone with known anaphylaxis.

✦ take care

Botulism in oil. Fresh garlic or fresh herbs in oil can grow botulism. Without refrigeration, never make them. The safe home method uses thoroughly dried herbs: heat the oil to 180°F, pour it over the dried material, cap and invert the container for 5 minutes, store it cool and dark, and use within 3 months (UMaine Bulletin #4385; Penn State).

Parasites, medicine stockpiles and dental care

Scabies needs permethrin 5% cream from the neck down, washed off after 8–14 hours. Without a washing machine, keep the person's bedding and clothing away from body contact for more than 72 hours (CDC).

Head lice can be removed with wet combing, which needs no drugs. Permethrin 1% is first-line where lice aren't resistant (AAFP). Stockpile a metal nit comb and permethrin.

Medicines past their expiry date. The FDA's Shelf Life Extension Program tests federal stockpiles. It found 88% of lots stayed stable at least a year past expiration, by an average of 66 months, but only in original packaging under controlled storage. The FDA does not endorse relying on expired drugs from home cabinets (Lyon et al. 2006; FDA). Store medicines cool, dark and sealed. Keep a written list of each prescription's dose, frequency and allergies, and extra OTC medicines (Ready.gov).

Dental emergencies were not researched. Print Hesperian's free Where There Is No Dentist and Where There Is No Doctor (Hesperian).

The Maker's Art

Cordage (reverse wrap) B

  1. Gather a fiber bundle, divide it into two strands, and twist one strand away from you until it kinks.
  2. Wrap that strand over the other in the direction opposite to the twist. If twist and wrap go the same way, the cord unravels.
  3. Repeat with alternating strands, adding fiber before a strand runs thin.
  4. For heavy cord, reverse-wrap two finished cords together.

(Wilderness College; Primitive Ways)

Soap

Lye from hardwood ash B.

  1. Pour hot water through hardwood ash. Softwood ash is too resinous.
  2. Collect the brown liquid.
  3. Test it: it is strong enough when a fresh egg or potato floats with part of it showing above the surface. Accounts range from a quarter of the shell showing to about half. If it sinks, pour the liquid back through the ash or boil it down (Classic Bells; Journey to Forever).
  4. Never eat the test egg.

Ash lye is mostly potash, so it makes soft soap. Boil fat with lye, adding lye a little at a time until the mixture stops feeling greasy (Mother Earth News).

Cold-process tallow soap with stockpiled lye (NaOH) B.

  • Recipe: 30 oz beef tallow + 3.88 oz NaOH + 11 oz distilled water, for an 8% superfat (Prairie Homestead).
  • Weigh everything; don't measure by volume.
  • Saponification values (NaOH): tallow 0.143, lard 0.141 (Soap Making 101).
  • Cure at least 6 weeks. Hot-process soap is usable in 1–2 weeks (Lovely Greens).
  • Stockpiling sodium hydroxide makes soap far more predictable than ash lye.
✦ take care

Lye. Wear goggles, rubber gloves, long sleeves and closed shoes. Add lye to water, don't lean over the container, ventilate the area, and never use aluminum or tin containers (Healthline). Flush skin with plenty of water C.

Plant soaps A/B. Peel soaproot (Chlorogalum) bulbs or yucca roots, crush or grate them, and agitate them in water to make a lather (USDA FS; Rural Sprout). Saponins are toxic to swallow and were used traditionally as fish poisons. Keep saponin wash water out of streams.

Pottery B

  1. Mix dug clay with water and decant it repeatedly to remove sand and silt. Drain the clay in a cloth bag (Ceramic Arts Network).
  2. Test plasticity: roll a coil and bend it. Raw local clay often cracks.
  3. Add temper at about 1 part sand, crushed quartz, burned crushed shell or grog (ground old pottery) to 2 parts clay. Temper cuts shrinkage cracking (Outdoor Life; Palomar College).
  4. Make pinch pots first, then coil pots.
  5. Dry the pieces at least 24 hours, and ideally several weeks.
  6. Pit-fire slowly. Nest the pots in a pit with wood, shavings, dried manure and leaves, and raise the heat gradually. Even dry pots explode from fast heating, and a cold gust can crack them. Pit firing reaches roughly 1,100°C and produces earthenware (Wikipedia: Pit fired pottery).

Earthenware is porous. It suits dry storage and zeer coolers but leaks liquids unless it is burnished or sealed C.

Containers A/B

  • Birch bark: harvest in June–July when it peels easily, and get permission first (UAF Alaska Ethnobotany). A container folded from one sheet is seamless and leak-proof (Canadian Museum of History). Stitched forms are sewn with split root (U. Maine Hudson Museum).
  • Hard-shell gourds (Lagenaria): cure up to 6 months somewhere dark, dry and ventilated, turning them every couple of weeks. Scrape surface mold off gourds that stay hard. Discard soft or seeping ones (Purdue). Clean them with 1:9 bleach, and seal the inside with beeswax (Garden Guides).

Tools

  • Knapping flint, chert or obsidian A/B. Wear goggles, gloves and a leather lap and hand pad. A 2023 study documents cuts, infection and eye loss (Cambridge Core). Knap outdoors or wet, because repeated silica dust exposure risks silicosis (Puget Sound Knappers). Collect and bury the sharp debris.
  • Bone and antler B. Groove at least halfway through, support the piece, then snap it. A bone awl takes about 1.5 hours with stone tools (Primitive Ways).
  • Ground stone axe A/B. Peck the shape with a harder hammerstone such as jasper or quartzite, then grind. Experiments took about 36–60 hours (Journal of Lithic Studies). It is a winter project. Scavenged steel is far faster.
  • Selfbow A/B. Beginners should use hickory, ash, hackberry or elm, not Osage orange (MDC). Split staves rather than sawing them, season the wood (green-wood bows take set and fail), and tiller slowly by removing belly wood until both limbs bend evenly (Wikipedia: Self bow; Outdoor Life).

Brain-tanned buckskin B

  1. Flesh the hide: remove meat, fat and membrane.
  2. Grain it: scrape off the hair and the grain layer.
  3. Dress it: soak 2–8 hours in 1 deer brain mashed into 1 gallon of hot water. Where chronic wasting disease is present, use about a dozen egg yolks per gallon of warm water instead.
  4. Wring it out, then work it continuously while it dries until it is soft.
  5. Smoke it. An unsmoked hide that gets wet goes back to rawhide.

(Outdoor Life; Wilderness College; Alaska Dept. of Fish & Game)

Glue B

Pine pitch glue is melted resin plus powdered charcoal, anywhere from 3:1 to 1:1 resin to charcoal by volume. More charcoal makes it harder but more brittle. A common variant is 4 sap : 1 charcoal : 1 powdered rabbit droppings (Puget Sound Knappers; Wildwood Survival). Lab work confirms that charcoal and gentle heating strengthen it, up to a point (Leiden University). Resin is flammable, so melt it gently.

Hide glue comes from boiling clean, lean hide. Cowhide worked; pig hide was too fatty (EXARC).

Wards Against Rot

Preserving and cooking food without power
XIII · Death🜔Salt♄Saturn

Decay is a hungry spirit. Salt, smoke, sun, acid and steam are the wards against it, and the gravest of the rot-spirits, botulism, laughs at a mere boil.

◉ Ouija Cat, by telepathy

Botulism doesn't care about your grandmother's recipe. Pressure-can the beans or don't can them.

Canning: the deadliest kitchen mistake A

Low-acid foods (pH above 4.6) include meat, poultry, seafood, milk, most vegetables and mixtures. They must be pressure-canned at 240–250°F (10–15 psi), because botulinum spores survive boiling. In the right conditions they can make toxin within 3–4 days. A boiling-water canner would need 7–11 hours to match a pressure canner, so it is not an option for these foods.

Boiling-water canning is only for acid foods: fruit, pickles, sauerkraut, jams. Acidify tomatoes and figs with lemon juice or citric acid. Store jars below 95°F, and ideally at 50–70°F (NCHFP).

Open-kettle, oven and "dry canning" are unsafe C. Without a pressure canner, dry, ferment, salt, smoke-cook or root-cellar low-acid foods instead.

Sauerkraut (NCHFP) A

  1. Shred 5 lb of cabbage and mix it with 3 Tbsp canning or pickling salt.
  2. Pack it down until juice covers it. If juice doesn't cover the cabbage, add brine at 1½ Tbsp salt per quart of boiled, cooled water.
  3. Keep the cabbage submerged.
  4. Ferment at 70–75°F for 3–4 weeks, or at 60–65°F for 5–6 weeks. Below 60°F it may not ferment; above 75°F it goes soft.
  5. If using jar weights, skim scum 2–3 times a week.

(NCHFP Sauerkraut)

Jerky and pemmican

  • Jerky A. Slice the meat ¼ in thick or less. Heat it to 160°F (poultry 165°F) before drying, then dry at a steady 130–140°F (USDA FSIS). Alternatively, after drying, heat the strips 10 minutes in a 275°F oven (K-State MF3173). Bacteria get more heat-resistant once dry, so the kill step comes first.
  • Pemmican B. Mix pounded dried meat with rendered fat at about 1:1 by weight. Use more fat in cold weather, and optionally add dried fruit (onX; MeatEater).

Drying, smoking and salting A

  • Sun drying is for fruit only, on days of 85°F or more with humidity below 60% (NCHFP; Virginia Tech).
    • Afterward, pasteurize against insects: 0°F for 48 hours, or 160°F for 30 minutes in trays no more than 1 in deep.
    • The best drying temperature is about 140°F. Hotter air cooks the surface and invites mold.
    • In humid regions, use a vented glass-covered solar cabinet. The UC Davis chimney dryer dries even under partial cloud (UC Davis).
  • Smoking. Cold smoking (below about 80–90°F) leaves food raw, and food scientists "cannot recommend" it. Hot-smoked fish must reach 150°F internal for 30 minutes after brining (Oregon State Seafood; Food Safety News).
  • Salt curing. Figures conflict: about 2–3% salt in the finished product, against 3–4% of product weight applied as a dry cure (Oklahoma State; The Culinary Pro). Long dry cures need nitrite or nitrate (Cure #1 is 6.25% sodium nitrite) to prevent botulism (USDA FSIS). Stockpile curing salt if you plan to cure meat, or treat cured meat as raw and cook it.

Potatoes: cure them for 2 weeks at 50–60°F and 85–90% humidity, then store at about 40°F. Below 40°F they turn sweet. Grain:

  • Store it below 12–13% moisture. Most insects need 13–15% moisture to thrive (U. Kentucky).
  • Keep it below 60°F, where insects stop feeding (U. Delaware).
  • Add about 500 cc of oxygen absorber per 5-gallon bucket (USU Extension). Other sources give different figures.

Zeer pot (pot-in-pot cooler) A/B. Set one unglazed clay pot inside another with wet sand between them, and cover it with a wet cloth. It cools 8–15°C below ambient and can extend vegetable life up to 5 times, but only in dry air. Expect it to work well in the Southwest and poorly in the humid Southeast (UC Davis; Engineering for Change).

Cooking without power

Food safety A. Bacteria multiply fastest between 40°F and 140°F and can double in 20 minutes, so keep hot food at 140°F or above (USDA FSIS). Wild game goes to 165°F (Section 5).

Rocket stove B. Larry Winiarski's principles, developed at Aprovecho:

  • Insulate the combustion chamber with light, heat-resistant material such as perlite or wood ash.
  • Put an insulated chimney above the fire, about 3× as tall as it is wide.
  • Burn only the tips of sticks, feeding them in gradually.
  • Keep good airflow through the fuel and an even cross-section all the way through.
  • Fit an insulated skirt around the pot. In Aprovecho tests, a skirt raised one stove's efficiency from 21% to 39%.

(Aprovecho; Wikipedia)

Box solar oven B.

  • It reaches about 250–300°F, up to about 390°F with good reflectors.
  • Cook between 10 am and 2 pm on clear days, and add about 1 hour to normal times.
  • For about 4 lb of food: rice, fruit and above-ground vegetables take 1–2 hours; potatoes, root vegetables and most bread take 3–4 hours; most dried beans take 5–8 hours.
  • It is useless on overcast days.

(Solar Cookers International via Scribd; Mother Earth News)

Dutch oven B. For about 325–350°F, put the oven's diameter in inches +3 briquettes on top and −3 underneath. A 12-inch oven gets 15 on top and 9 below. Wind, cold and altitude change results (MDC; Field & Stream).

Haybox (retained-heat cooker) B. It saves fuel, but food can sink into the danger zone.

  1. Boil the food at least 5 minutes before boxing it.

(LowImpact/Aprovecho)

Stars, Shadows and the Hour

Time and direction: a stick, the sun and Polaris
X · Wheel of Fortune🜁Air☉Sun

The heavens are the oldest clock and the oldest compass. A stick, a shadow, the fixed star in the north: with these the lost are never truly lost.

◉ Ouija Cat, by telepathy

You have never once looked up. The sky has been holding the clock this entire time.

Finding direction

Shadow-stick method A. It works anywhere on Earth.

  1. Plant a stick about 1 m tall upright on level ground.
  2. Mark the tip of its shadow.
  3. Wait 10–15 minutes and mark the tip again.
  4. The first mark is always west and the second is east.
  5. A line at right angles to the west–east line runs north–south.

The Army standard is accuracy within 15° (Army Study Guide; MDC Xplor). Around local solar noon, the shortest shadow points true north anywhere in the continental US C.

Watch method (Northern Hemisphere) A/B. Point the hour hand at the sun. South lies halfway between the hour hand and 12, so north is opposite. Use 1 instead of 12 during daylight saving time C. It is less accurate than the shadow stick and can be badly off at low latitudes (Army Study Guide; NPS). Some printed versions wrongly say north lies halfway; this guide uses the corrected wording.

Polaris A. Follow the two "pointer" stars at the end of the Big Dipper's cup (Dubhe and Merak) about 5 times their separation to reach Polaris, which marks north. It is the end of the Little Dipper's handle (NASA; EarthSky). Cassiopeia's "W" sits opposite the Dipper across Polaris and works as a backup (Space.com). Polaris's height above the horizon roughly equals your latitude C. You can use that to set a sundial with no map.

Compass and declination A. Declination is the angle between magnetic and true north. Salt Lake City's is about 12.5° E, for example (Utah Geological Survey). True bearing = magnetic bearing + declination, with west declination counted as negative (NWCG). Look up your declination now on NOAA's calculator and write it on your compass and maps.

Improvised compass B.

  1. Stroke a needle in one direction, about a dozen times on a magnet or about 100 times on silk.
  2. Float it on a leaf in still water, sheltered from wind and away from metal.
  3. It aligns north–south, but it doesn't show which end is north, so check it against the sun or Polaris. Many needles come already magnetized (Wildwood Survival; Field & Stream).

Moss grows where it is damp and shady, not reliably on the north side C.

Building a horizontal sundial A

  1. Find true north by the shadow stick at solar noon or by Polaris.
  2. Make a flat, level dial plate. Draw a noon line pointing true north.
  3. Cut a triangular gnomon whose sloping edge (the "style") rises at an angle equal to your latitude. Mount it on the noon line with the high end toward north (Schoolphysics).
  4. Draw each hour line at angle θ from the noon line, where tan θ = sin(latitude) × tan(15° × hours from noon) (North American Sundial Society). Morning lines go on the west side and afternoon lines on the east, mirrored.

Converting sundial time to clock time A. A sundial shows local solar time. To get clock time, apply three corrections:

  1. Longitude: correct by 4 minutes per degree between you and your time zone's central meridian (75°W Eastern, 90°W Central, 105°W Mountain, 120°W Pacific). A dial west of its meridian reads early (NASS).
  2. Equation of time: the sun runs up to 16½ minutes fast around November 3 and 14 minutes slow around February 11 (Wikipedia: Equation of time).
  3. Daylight saving: add 1 hour (Penn State EME 810).

In a long collapse, solar time itself may become the community standard. Local noon is simply when the shadow is shortest.

Equatorial sundial (simpler) A.

  1. Tilt a flat plate to 90° minus your latitude, so the plate is parallel to the equator and a rod through its center points at Polaris.
  2. Mark hour lines evenly every 15°.
  3. Mark both faces. The top face is lit from the spring to the fall equinox and the bottom face the rest of the year. Numbers run clockwise on top and counterclockwise underneath.

(The Sundial Primer; British Sundial Society)

Big Dipper star clock B. Imagine a clock hand running from Polaris through the pointer stars. It turns counterclockwise 15° per hour. Around March 6 it reads the actual time. On other dates it drifts about 4 minutes a day, or 2 hours a month. This handbook's correction, which you should verify, is to subtract about 2 hours for each month after March 6, then add 1 hour for daylight saving (Sky & Telescope; timeanddate).

Seasonal calendar C. Mark the noon shadow tip of a fixed post each day. The shadow is longest at the winter solstice and shortest at the summer solstice, and sunrise points on the horizon reach their extremes at the same times.

Reading the weather A

  • "Red sky at night, sailor's delight; red sky in morning, sailor's warning" holds "within limits" where weather moves west to east, as it does over most of the US. It fails when storms move south to north (Library of Congress).
  • A halo around the sun or moon comes from ice-crystal cloud and often means rain or snow soon (NRCC Cornell).
  • Approaching warm front: thin cirrus thickens to a cirrostratus veil, then lowers through altostratus to nimbostratus, bringing steady rain (UCAR). Cirrus may lead the front by 24–48 hours (secondary source, Fiveable).
  • Towering cumulonimbus means unstable air and thunderstorms (NOAA JetStream).
  • Barometer: watch the 3-hour "barometric tendency." A steady fall points to approaching low pressure (NWS).
  • Buys Ballot's law: in the Northern Hemisphere, stand with your back to the wind. Low pressure is to your left and slightly forward (Britannica).
  • Woolly bear caterpillar stripes reflect past growing conditions, not the coming winter (NRCC Cornell / NOAA).
  • Print the NWS cloud chart.

Contaminated water spreads dysentery, cholera, typhoid and hepatitis (Ready.gov). In a Maria-length outage of 68 days without water, sanitation becomes a top disease-prevention job.

Twin-bucket toilet A. Pacific Northwest emergency agencies adapted it from New Zealand's "twin no-mix" design. It costs $20 or less and beats bag, single-bucket and pit options (Clallam County WA).

  1. Use one bucket for urine and one for feces. Mixing them causes most of the odor.
  2. Cover each deposit of feces with about ½ cup of sawdust, mulch or other carbon material.
  3. Urine is low-risk. Dilute it and pour it 6–8 in below the soil surface.
  4. Set up two handwashing stations, one at the toilet and one at food preparation.

(Oregon State EM 9334; Washington County OR)

The full manual is the RDPO Emergency Toilet Guidebook (Wilsonville OR).

Latrines and cat holes. Standards range from 100 ft to 200 ft from water. WEDC says at least 40 m where ground conditions are uncertain (Indiana DOH; WEDC). Use one rule: at least 200 ft (60 m) from any well, spring or stream, and downhill of it. Dig cat holes 6–8 in deep (Wikipedia citing Leave No Trace). Install a handwashing device at every latrine (Army MD0161).

Tippy tap A.

  1. Hang a 5 L (1.5 gal) jug with a handle from a crossbar, by string threaded through two holes in its back.
  2. Tie a stick pedal so pressing it tips the jug and water pours from a small hole, leaving hands free.
  3. Clean the outside daily and disinfect the inside weekly.

(CDC archived; Special Olympics)

Composting human waste long-term A/B. The extension pathogen benchmark comes from livestock composting: 131°F or above for 3 days, with turning (Oregon State EM 8825). Joseph Jenkins's Humanure Handbook adds curing for at least a year. It also notes that most composting toilets never get hot enough (Humanure Handbook ch. 8). Check the pile with a thermometer. As a precaution, use finished compost on trees and non-food areas C.

◉ Ouija Cat, by telepathy

Neighbors beat bunkers

Disaster sociology is consistent: across more than 535 field studies, ordinary people mostly cooperate. Looting during the emergency period is uncommon, and much of what gets called looting is people taking food for family when payment systems fail (UD Disaster Research Center). After Katrina, much of the harm came from authorities and vigilantes acting on rumor (The Week on Solnit).

Daniel Aldrich studied recoveries in Tokyo, Kobe, Tamil Nadu and New Orleans. Even after controlling for damage, density and wealth, pre-disaster social capital was "the strongest and most robust predictor" of recovery (Aldrich).

Puerto Rico's community kitchens grew into Centros de Apoyo Mutuo (mutual aid centers). Members gave time, skills or food in exchange for meals, and the centers were still running through Hurricane Fiona in 2022 (NPR).

Economic collapses grind slowly:

  • Argentina, 2001: "cash is definitely king" in the first stage, when banks and ATMs fail, and barter clubs formed (Survival Podcast; Resilience.org).
  • Venezuela, 2016: about 74% of survey respondents reported losing weight, an average of about 19 lb (UPI).
  • Sarajevo, 1992–96: parks became corn plots, residents leaned on wild plants, and survival varied by neighborhood access to water and garden land (FAMA; Redžić 2010).

Skills that trade well, judging from what mutual-aid centers and history show was in demand C: group cooking, first aid, repair and mechanics, gardening and seed saving, radio, and childcare.

Low-profile security and de-escalation

Police home-security advice makes a house look occupied and not worth targeting, without looking fortified A:

  • Deadbolts; pins or dowels in sliding doors (Albuquerque PD).
  • Shrubs under 3 ft and trees trimmed up to 7 ft so sightlines stay clear (Roseville PD).
  • No valuables visible from windows.
  • Neighbors watching each other's homes (Liberty MO PD).

In a grid-down period C:

  • hang blackout curtains if you have light when neighbors don't,
  • don't advertise stockpiles or generator noise, and
  • share through organized neighbor channels.

De-escalation A: use a calm voice and open body language. Learn the person's name and restate what they say so they feel heard. Never tell anyone to "calm down" (CISA; RTDNA).

Radio A.

  • FRS needs no license.
  • GMRS needs an FCC license, with no exam and a 10-year term. Any GMRS channel may be used in emergencies (FCC).
  • Ham radio needs an exam through a local club and can reach far beyond your neighborhood.
  • Choose radios that run on AA batteries (Polk County OR).

Growing food: the first year must be bridged

Gardens take a season or more to produce. Stored food must carry you through year one C.

For calories, potatoes lead at roughly 17.8–26 million kcal per acre, followed by corn and rice. Sweet potatoes produce high calories per unit of time and area, and wheat and soybeans trail far behind (Mathscinotes; Wikipedia: Staple food). Backyard yields fall well short of commercial ones.

A practical no-input plan C:

  • potatoes and sweet potatoes for calories,
  • dry beans for protein and nitrogen,
  • flint or dent corn and winter squash for storable calories,
  • saved seed, and
  • compost and rabbit manure for fertility.

Three Sisters (Haudenosaunee/Iroquois system, via Cornell Cooperative Extension) A. Native peoples across North America used many variations. Southeastern nations such as the Choctaw did not always plant all three in one mound (Almanac). Cornell's method (Cornell CCE):

  1. Choose a site with 6–8 hours of sun. Plant after frost, once nights reach about 50°F, and no later than June.
  2. Build mounds about 12 in high and 18 in–3 ft across. In dry areas, flatten the top into a basin.
  3. Soak 4–7 corn seeds (dent, flint or flour types, which store as grain) for no more than 8 hours. Plant them about 6 in apart in the center and thin to 3–4.
  4. When the corn is 6 in tall, plant 6 pole beans in a ring about 6 in out and thin to 3–4. Bush beans won't climb.
  5. Plant 4 squash or pumpkin seeds about 1 ft beyond the beans and thin to 1.

Seed saving A. Only open-pollinated seed, which includes heirlooms, breeds true. F1 hybrids do not, so check the packet for "F1" (U. Arizona Extension).

  • Beans self-pollinate and are the easiest crop to save. Isolate them 40–100 ft.
  • Tomatoes rarely cross.
  • Summer squash, pumpkins and acorn squash are often the same species and cross readily. Isolate them up to half a mile, or hand-pollinate (UC ANR; Seed Savers Exchange).

Livestock A.

  • Rabbits: a backyard herd of 4–5 does takes about 60 hours a year, under 15 minutes a day (UMaine #1044). Fryers are usually butchered by 10 weeks, on feed of 16–18% protein (Oregon State).
  • Hens: a 6 lb hen eats about 3 lb of feed a week and starts laying around 6 months. She lays about 6 eggs a week at peak, needs 12–14 hours of light to keep laying, and needs 3–5 sq ft of coop space, with one nest box per 4–5 hens (UMN Extension).
  • Expect few winter eggs without electric light. Bought feed will eventually have to be replaced by forage, insects, garden scraps and home-grown grain C.
  • Check milk-animal pastures for white snakeroot (Section 5).

This research changes the picture of grid-down survival in one main way. The movie threats, such as marauders, mass panic and wild plants as an easy pantry, are not what history shows. The real threats are mundane and preventable:

  • a generator in the garage,
  • an elderly neighbor alone in a cold house,
  • a missed insulin refill,
  • a dirty water jug,
  • a carrot-family plant picked by someone who was nearly sure.

The evidence-backed skills here are boiling and dosing water, stopping bleeding, keeping wounds open, rewarming gently, pressure-canning low-acid food or not canning it at all, and separating toilet waste. They do more for survival than any primitive craft, and every one can be learned this month.

Two further points follow. First, many of the most refined techniques here, such as the earth lodge, chickee, Three Sisters mound, acorn leaching, bow drill and buckskin, come from Indigenous nations who adapted them to these exact landscapes over centuries. The best way to learn them is from those communities, while they can still teach in person. Second, the weakest evidence in this handbook sits in the places people trust most: herbal remedies, foraged mushrooms and the edibility test. Use those sections with the most caution, and lean on community and practice most of all. A household that has practiced its bow drill, test-fired its adobe bricks, cooked a solar meal and met its neighbors is far better prepared than a better-stocked household that has done none of these.
